# Valuing cyclical sectors: P/E traps, the capital cycle and lasting returns on capital

- **Source:** Desk research, 2026-11-22: practitioner frameworks (Peter Lynch, Marathon Asset Management, McKinsey). Sources are linked inline and listed at the end.
- **Added:** 2026-11-22
- **Tags:** research, valuation, cyclicals, capital cycle, ROIC, P/B
- **Relevant models here:** Nifty sectors oscillator (C1 primary metric per index, C12 secondary, C15 ROE vs own range, Sector cycle modules); Nifty 200 stocks model.

> Not investment advice.

## 1. For cyclicals, a low P/E is often a warning

- Peter Lynch's well-known rule for cyclicals (metals, autos, cement, commodity chemicals, PSU banks) runs against ordinary P/E logic:
  - A **low P/E often marks the top**, because earnings are at a peak that won't last.
  - A **high P/E can mark the bottom**, because earnings are depressed and about to recover.
- He warned that buying a cyclical after several years of record earnings, when the P/E has hit a low, is a fast way to lose half your money (summarised by [Gurufocus](https://www.gurufocus.com/news/1863600/peter-lynch-how-to-invest-in-cyclical-stocks) and [Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/investing/value/2007/01/24/investing-in-cyclicals.aspx); check *One Up on Wall Street* for exact wording).
- **Fix:** value cyclicals on measures that don't swing with the earnings cycle:
  - price to book;
  - EV/EBITDA on mid-cycle margins;
  - P/E on normalised (average-cycle) earnings.
- McKinsey's work on [valuing cyclical companies](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/how-to-value-cyclical-companies) makes a related point: analyst forecasts for cyclicals tend to miss the turns, and share prices move ahead of the earnings cycle. The article itself couldn't be retrieved; this is from its summary.
- **Graphite electrodes, 2017–19** (from the [sector rotation note](sector-rotation-7-indian-bull-runs.md)): margins rose from about 3% to 64% and net profit from about ₹70 crore to ₹3,400 crore in two years. The P/E looked cheap at the peak, and the stocks then fell for years.

**How the board handles it:**
- The sectors model already uses P/B as the main metric for banks, PSU banks, metal, energy and realty (design §2).
- It adds a secondary metric (C12) and ROE against the index's own range (C15), so a cheap-looking P/E on peak earnings is checked against book value and peak returns.

## 2. The capital cycle (Marathon Asset Management)

[*Capital Returns: Investing Through the Capital Cycle*](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/financial-analysts-journal/2016/capital-returns-investing-through-the-capital-cycle), edited by Edward Chancellor, collects Marathon's letters from 2002 to 2015.

**The loop:**
1. High returns attract capital.
2. Supply expands.
3. Returns fall.
4. Capital leaves.
5. Supply shrinks.
6. Returns recover.

**What Marathon watches:**
- **The supply side more than demand.** Demand is hard to forecast and everyone forecasts it; supply changes slowly and is visible in capex plans, IPOs and new entrants.
- **Management's capital allocation.** Good managers stop expanding near the peak and invest at the trough.
- **Industry consolidation.** Fewer, more disciplined players lift returns even when demand is flat.
- **Investment bankers as a signal.** Their busiest periods (IPOs, secondary issues, mergers) cluster near sector peaks ([CFA Institute review](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/blogs/enterprising-investor/2016/book-review-capital-returns), [MOI Global](https://moiglobal.com/lattice-work-capital-cycle-theory)).

The academic evidence for the supply side is in the [froth note](sector-froth-signals.md).

**Where Indian sectors sit** (late 2026, the presenter's view from the [sector rotation note](sector-rotation-7-indian-bull-runs.md)):

| Stage | Sectors |
|---|---|
| High returns | Hotels |
| Capital flooding in | Transformers, solar, smart meters |
| Turning | Cables, co-working spaces |
| Consolidating after a bust | Paints, water EPC |

## 3. Returns on capital by industry last for decades

- [McKinsey, "A long-term look at ROIC"](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/a-long-term-look-at-roic): differences in return on invested capital between industries persist for decades. Software and services, for example, had a median ROIC of 18% from 1963 to 2004.
- Differences *within* an industry can be larger than between industries.
- **Mobility:** companies earning more than 20% had about a 50% chance of still doing so ten years later; companies at 5–10% had about 40%.
- Returns partly converge towards the average, but the gap never fully closes ([Balancing ROIC and growth](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/balancing-roic-and-growth-to-build-value)).

**What it means:**
- Structurally high-return sectors (FMCG, IT services, private banks' best franchises) deserve higher normal multiples.
- That is why the sectors model scores each index against its *own* 10-year valuation range rather than against the market.
- The exception is a structural break, when a sector's normal changes. Example: paints after new entrants arrived in 2024–25, where the old range may overstate fair value.

## What this means for the board

Triaged in the [board backlog](board-backlog.md) (research batch, 2026-11-22):

- **Already covered:** P/B for cyclicals (C1), a secondary metric (C12), ROE against own range (C15), own-history anchors.
- **Board candidate:** a **"peak margin" guard** for cyclical families. When operating margin is in the top decile of its own 10-year history *and* P/E is in the bottom quartile, treat the low P/E as not cheap (cap the valuation contribution). Blocker: needs a 10-year sector margin history.
- **Knowledge only:** capital-cycle stage calls for industries outside the board's indices.
